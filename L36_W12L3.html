Inside an Ethernet cable are four pairs. Twisting makes noise hit both wires of a pair almost equally, and the receiver only cares about the difference, so the noise cancels. Different twist rates stop pairs interfering with each other. That is why, at the plug, you untwist as little as possible.

Example 1 — categories. Cat5e is enough for gigabit to 100 m: our whole lab. Cat6 has a plastic spline and tighter twists for 10 gigabit over short runs. Cat6 costs more and is stiffer; it is not 'faster' for us.

Example 2 — straight vs crossover. A straight-through lead is T568B on both ends. A crossover (B one end, A the other) was for PC-to-PC without a switch. Modern NICs auto-detect (Auto-MDIX), so straight-through works everywhere now.

Example 3 — the law. In Australia, cabling fixed in a building (wall sockets, ceiling runs, patch panels) must be done by an ACMA-registered cabler. Making and plugging in a patch lead is fine. In this lab you only ever plug into the lab switch and your own PCs, never the school's wall ports.

Words

Twisted pair
Two wires twisted together so interference affects both equally and cancels.
Cat5e / Cat6
Cable categories: 5e does 1 Gb/s to 100 m; 6 does 10 Gb/s to 55 m.
RJ45 / 8P8C
The 8-pin plug on the end of an Ethernet lead.
Patch lead
A short cable with plugs on both ends; fine for anyone to make and use.
Registered cabler
In Australia, the licensed person who may install fixed cabling in walls and ceilings.
